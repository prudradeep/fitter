# Methodology for Hazard and Mitigation Measure Clarification and Validation

## 1. Purpose

This methodology defines how user-proposed regional hazards and mitigation measures are clarified, validated, accepted, rejected, or returned for revision within a guided twin-transition policy analysis.

The methodology separates two different activities:

1. **Clarification** determines whether the user's intended meaning can be understood reliably.
2. **Validation** determines whether the clarified input is relevant, non-duplicative, and supported or contradicted by the applicable evidence base.

Clarification does not prove that an input is correct. Validation must not silently reinterpret an unclear input. This separation prevents a plausible interpretation generated by the system from being mistaken for the user's actual proposal.

## 2. Common Methodological Principles

### 2.1 Context anchoring

Every assessment is anchored to the selected:

- country;
- region, where applicable;
- policy sector;
- hazard, for mitigation measure assessment; and
- affected socio-demographic profiles, where relevant.

Material from another geography or sector may be treated as labelled background, but it must not be represented as direct evidence for the selected context unless the source supports that transfer.

### 2.2 Meaning before evidence

The workflow first establishes that an input is understandable. Evidence assessment occurs only after the intended meaning is sufficiently clear.

### 2.3 Permissive treatment of meaningful regional knowledge

User-added regional hazards may extend the predefined sector hazard list. A proposed hazard is not rejected merely because it is new, locally specific, or absent from the survey hazard list.

### 2.4 Evidence discipline

Evidence is optional at hazard intake and mitigation justification stages. When evidence is supplied, it must contain readable supporting content. A filename or web address without extractable content is not treated as usable evidence.

### 2.5 No invented support

Validation uses only the designated authoritative material. General knowledge, plausibility, and user assertions are not treated as external evidence.

### 2.6 Transparent uncertainty

Absence of support is distinguished from contradiction:

- **Supported** means the authoritative material meets the relevant assessment test.
- **Contradicted** means the authoritative material actively conflicts with the proposal.
- **Insufficient information** means the material neither supports nor contradicts the proposal adequately.

### 2.7 Graceful failure

If a required language model, retrieval service, or validation service is unavailable, the input is not automatically accepted or rejected. The workflow reports that validation is unavailable and preserves or requests the relevant user input for a later attempt.

# Part A: Hazard Clarification and Validation Workflow

## 3. Objective of Hazard Assessment

The hazard workflow determines whether a user-proposed regional hazard:

- expresses a recognizable negative event, risk, or outcome;
- is sufficiently clear to be assessed;
- is not already covered by an existing hazard;
- is relevant to the selected country, region, and sector;
- is compatible with the authoritative sector statistical context; and
- has a meaningful justification.

## 4. Required and Optional Hazard Inputs

### 4.1 Required inputs

- A proposed hazard description.
- A reason explaining why the hazard is relevant to the selected context.

### 4.2 Optional inputs

- A published web source.
- An uploaded PDF or word-processing document.
- Other readable evidence content.

## 5. Hazard Workflow Overview

The hazard workflow proceeds through the following stages:

1. Receive the proposed hazard.
2. Conduct basic input screening.
3. conduct exact and close-overlap screening.
4. Conduct semantic clarity and overlap review.
5. Request a reason and optional evidence.
6. Conduct reason and evidence quality review.
7. Retrieve authoritative sector statistical context.
8. Validate contextual compatibility.
9. Accept and record the hazard, or return it for revision.

## 6. Stage 1: Hazard Input Screening

The proposed hazard is first checked for the presence of meaningful text.

The input is returned for revision when it is:

- blank;
- random characters;
- keyboard mashing;
- gibberish;
- unrecognizable text;
- too short to determine intent;
- only a broad topic label; or
- too incomplete to identify a risk mechanism or negative outcome.

Grammar, spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and style errors do not by themselves invalidate a hazard when the intended risk is understandable.

## 7. Stage 2: Hazard Clarification Review

The proposed hazard is classified as **valid**, **ambiguous**, or **invalid**.

### 7.1 Valid

A hazard is considered clear enough to proceed when it expresses a meaningful hazard-like phrase, risk event, or negative outcome. It may be concise and does not need to specify every affected group, implementation detail, or geographic detail.

### 7.2 Ambiguous

A hazard is ambiguous when the text is recognizable but the intended risk cannot be determined. Examples include:

- a broad sector label without an outcome;
- an incomplete phrase;
- a proposal with no identifiable mechanism;
- a phrase that could refer to several materially different risks.

For an ambiguous hazard, the user is asked to rewrite it with a narrower mechanism or affected outcome. The workflow does not infer and save a meaning on the user's behalf.

### 7.3 Invalid

A hazard is invalid at intake when it has no clear meaning or is already substantially covered by an existing hazard.

## 8. Stage 3: Duplicate and Scope-Overlap Assessment

Duplicate assessment occurs before contextual validation so that users do not spend time justifying a hazard already represented in the analysis.

### 8.1 Comparison set

The proposal is compared with:

- predefined sector hazards; and
- previously accepted user-added regional hazards in the current analysis context.

### 8.2 Duplicate criteria

A proposal is treated as already covered when it is:

- an exact match;
- a close paraphrase;
- the same hazard expressed in another language;
- a broader or narrower restatement with substantially overlapping risk;
- a differently worded description of the same mechanism; or
- a differently worded description of substantially the same affected outcome.

### 8.3 Non-duplicate criterion

Two hazards are not treated as duplicates merely because they belong to the same broad topic. The mechanism or affected outcome must substantially overlap.

### 8.4 User response

When overlap is found:

- the hazard is not accepted;
- relevant existing hazards are presented to the user; and
- the user is asked to select an existing hazard or rewrite the proposal to express a distinct risk.

## 9. Stage 4: Reason and Evidence Collection

Once the proposed hazard passes intake clarification and duplicate screening, the user is asked to explain why it should be considered relevant.

The reason is mandatory because the hazard may extend beyond the predefined survey list. The reason provides the contextual link needed to assess a locally specific addition.

Evidence remains optional. If supplied, it is treated as additional support rather than a mandatory condition for acceptance.

## 10. Stage 5: Reason and Evidence Quality Review

Before statistical validation, the reason and any evidence are checked for usability.

### 10.1 Reason quality criteria

The reason must:

- be recognizable and meaningful;
- explain relevance to the proposed hazard;
- contain enough context to assess the claim;
- avoid being only a broad label; and
- avoid unsupported numerical claims presented as established facts.

### 10.2 Evidence quality criteria

Supplied evidence must:

- contain readable extracted content;
- be identifiable as a source or evidence passage; and
- be relevant enough to support assessment of the proposed hazard and reason.

Unreadable extraction notices, unsupported file types, bare filenames, and bare web addresses without readable content do not satisfy the evidence check.

### 10.3 Style tolerance

Understandable content is not rejected solely for wording or style problems. Where a rejection appears to be based only on style, meaningful text is allowed to proceed.

## 11. Stage 6: Authoritative Context Retrieval

The workflow retrieves relevant excerpts from the sector-specific statistical knowledge source.

Retrieval uses the combined meaning of:

- the proposed hazard;
- the user's reason;
- supplied evidence;
- the selected sector and geography; and
- the existing hazard list.

The retrieved sector excerpts are treated as the authoritative statistical context for hazard validation.

## 12. Stage 7: Hazard Contextual Validation

The proposed hazard, reason, and optional evidence are assessed against the retrieved sector context.

### 12.1 Acceptance criteria

A proposed hazard is accepted when:

- the hazard and reason are meaningful;
- it is relevant to the selected country and sector;
- it is compatible with the authoritative sector context;
- it does not confuse a predictor or affected-group characteristic with a hazard;
- it does not contradict established statistical findings; and
- any supplied evidence is compatible with the proposal.

The exact hazard does not need to appear in the authoritative source. A plausible regional extension may be accepted when it is compatible with the source and clearly justified.

### 12.2 Rejection criteria

A proposed hazard is rejected or returned for revision when:

- the reason is too vague or generic to evaluate;
- it is unrelated to the selected sector;
- it is unrelated to the selected country or regional context;
- it clearly contradicts the authoritative sector context;
- it treats a predictor as though it were a hazard;
- it invents unsupported numerical findings as facts; or
- supplied evidence conflicts with the hazard or reason.

## 13. Stage 8: Hazard Outcome and Recording

### 13.1 Accepted hazard

After acceptance:

- the hazard is added to the regional hazard list;
- the hazard description, reason, evidence, sector, and region are recorded;
- the accepted hazard becomes available for affected-population analysis; and
- the workflow proceeds to identify or confirm affected socio-demographic profiles.

### 13.2 Revision required

When clarification or validation fails:

- the reason for failure is explained;
- relevant existing hazards are shown when overlap exists;
- the user may revise the hazard, reason, or evidence; and
- no new hazard is recorded until validation succeeds.

### 13.3 Validation unavailable

When required validation services are unavailable:

- no acceptance decision is made;
- the user is informed that validation could not be completed; and
- the user may retry when services are available.

## 14. Hazard Workflow Decision Summary

| Decision point | Proceed when | Return or stop when |
|---|---|---|
| Input screening | The hazard is meaningful and recognizable | Blank, gibberish, too short, or only a broad topic |
| Duplicate review | The hazard expresses a materially distinct risk | Existing hazard substantially covers it |
| Reason review | The reason explains contextual relevance | Missing, vague, unrelated, or unusable |
| Evidence review | No evidence is supplied, or supplied evidence is readable | Supplied evidence cannot be read |
| Contextual validation | Proposal is relevant and compatible with sector statistics | Proposal contradicts context or cannot be meaningfully assessed |
| Recording | All required gates pass | Any required gate remains unresolved |

# Part B: Mitigation Measure Clarification and Validation Workflow

## 15. Objective of Mitigation Measure Assessment

The mitigation workflow determines whether a proposed measure:

- is a meaningful and distinct policy action;
- can be understood without inventing user intent;
- has an understandable justification;
- addresses the selected hazard;
- has a supported harm-reduction pathway;
- is not contradicted by the authoritative support corpus; and
- can be summarized using only validated inputs and supported claims.

## 16. Required and Optional Mitigation Inputs

### 16.1 Required inputs

- A mitigation measure describing the proposed action or intervention.
- A justification explaining why the measure should reduce the negative impact of the selected hazard.

### 16.2 Context used in assessment

- Selected country, region, and sector.
- Selected hazard.
- Identified affected socio-demographic profiles.

### 16.3 Optional inputs

- A published web source.
- An uploaded PDF or word-processing document.
- Other readable evidence content.

## 17. Mitigation Workflow Overview

The mitigation workflow proceeds through the following stages:

1. Receive the proposed measure.
2. Conduct basic quality screening.
3. Conduct duplicate assessment.
4. Collect the justification and optional evidence.
5. Check evidence readability.
6. Conduct iterative clarification.
7. Freeze the clarified meaning.
8. Select the authoritative support corpus.
9. Retrieve, rank, and filter support excerpts.
10. Conduct repeated dimension-level grounded validation.
11. Aggregate verdicts and calculate the overall outcome.
12. Produce an entailment-checked grounded synthesis.
13. Record accepted results and preserve validated evidence.

## 18. Stage 1: Mitigation Measure Input Screening

The measure must describe a recognizable policy action or intervention.

The proposal is returned for revision when it is:

- blank;
- gibberish or keyboard mashing;
- too short to identify an action;
- unrelated to reducing the selected hazard's negative impact; or
- too generic or incomplete to understand as a mitigation measure.

Minor language and style errors do not cause rejection when the action is understandable.

## 19. Stage 2: Mitigation Duplicate Assessment

Duplicate assessment is restricted to existing mitigation measures associated with the same selected hazard and analysis context.

### 19.1 Local similarity review

The proposal is compared for:

- exact normalized matches;
- one description substantially containing the other; and
- high overlap in meaningful terms.

### 19.2 Semantic review

The proposal is also assessed for:

- the same policy action expressed differently;
- a close paraphrase;
- the same meaning in another language; or
- a narrower or restated version already covered by an existing measure.

Measures are not treated as duplicates merely because they belong to the same policy area.

### 19.3 User choice after a duplicate indication

When a possible duplicate is identified, the user may:

- inspect the existing measure, its reason, and available evaluation information;
- stop and write a different measure; or
- continue creating the proposed measure despite the similarity.

Duplicate detection therefore informs the user and reduces unnecessary duplication without automatically preventing a deliberately distinct submission.

## 20. Stage 3: Justification and Evidence Intake

The user must explain how the measure is expected to reduce the selected hazard's negative impact for the affected profiles.

The justification must be meaningful and sufficiently detailed to identify the claimed pathway. A short or unclear reason is returned for revision.

Evidence is optional. When evidence is supplied, it must contain readable supporting content. Unreadable or unextractable evidence is returned before clarification begins.

## 21. Stage 4: Clarification Track

The clarification track assesses only whether the proposal can be understood. It does not determine whether the measure is correct, feasible, adequately evidenced, or supported.

The operating principle is to default to clear unless a specific unresolved ambiguity can be identified.

### 21.1 Clarification dimensions

#### A. Specificity

The measure is clear on specificity when the proposed action or instrument can be identified. Exact amounts, timing, delivery arrangements, and implementation details are not required at this stage.

#### B. Justification clarity

The justification is clear when it can be restated as one declarative sentence linking the measure to its intended effect on the hazard without inventing content.

An unsupported or debatable justification may still be clear. Support is assessed later.

#### C. Evidence identifiability

Evidence is clear when the referenced source or content can be identified. When no evidence is supplied, this dimension is considered clear.

### 21.2 Priority order

Only one unresolved dimension is clarified at a time, in this order:

1. specificity;
2. justification clarity;
3. evidence identifiability.

### 21.3 Clarification questions

For the active unresolved dimension, the user receives two or three short questions tied to the specific ambiguous phrase. Questions from other dimensions are deferred until the active ambiguity is resolved.

Typical clarification topics include:

- what action will be taken;
- who will receive the measure and under what circumstances;
- how the action is expected to reduce the hazard;
- why it is appropriate for the affected group;
- what evidence source is being referenced; and
- which finding in the source supports the proposal.

### 21.4 Clarification history

All clarification questions and answers form an authoritative clarification history.

Later clarification:

- extends or overrides ambiguous original wording;
- prevents the same resolved issue from being asked again; and
- is used to construct the final clarified input.

Clarification answers are not treated as evidence that the measure is supported. They clarify user intent only.

### 21.5 Clarification answer quality

A clarification answer must be understandable and meaningfully address the question. It is rejected when it consists of:

- random or repeated characters;
- unrecognizable text;
- unexplained acronyms or jargon;
- vague fragments; or
- statements that do not clarify the proposal.

Factual correctness and evidence support are deliberately excluded from this check.

### 21.6 Clarification limit

The clarification cycle is limited to five rounds. If an unambiguous interpretation still cannot be established after the limit:

- temporary evidence associated with the unresolved submission is discarded;
- the user is asked to resubmit the measure, justification, and evidence with more concrete wording; and
- grounded validation does not proceed.

## 22. Stage 5: Input Freezing

Once all clarification dimensions are clear, the workflow creates a concise and unambiguous representation of:

- the measure description;
- the justification; and
- the evidence reference or content.

This is the fixed interpretation used for validation. It integrates clarification history only to establish meaning and must not add new claims.

Evidence is preserved as an immutable source payload. It is not rewritten by the clarification process.

In limited cases, an input may be frozen after a substantive clarification answer when all other dimensions are clear and minimum meaningful-content checks are satisfied.

## 23. Stage 6: Authoritative Support Corpus Selection

The authoritative support corpus depends on whether the user supplied evidence.

### 23.1 User-evidence branch

When readable user evidence is supplied, validation uses only that evidence as the authoritative support corpus.

### 23.2 Curated-knowledge branch

When no user evidence is supplied, validation uses the curated main knowledge base as the authoritative support corpus.

The selected corpus is fixed for the verdict. The assessment must not introduce support from general knowledge, the user's assertions, sector statistics, or another corpus.

## 24. Stage 7: Retrieval, Ranking, and Evidence Eligibility

### 24.1 Retrieval query

Relevant support is retrieved using the combined meaning of:

- the selected hazard;
- the frozen measure description;
- the frozen justification;
- country;
- sector; and
- region.

The query prioritizes the intervention and its claimed mechanism. Long affected-profile lists are not used to dilute retrieval relevance.

### 24.2 Retrieval methods

Evidence retrieval combines:

- semantic similarity;
- lexical matching;
- phrase matching;
- source-title matching; and
- page-specific matching where applicable.

### 24.3 Reranking

Retrieved excerpts may be reranked by a dedicated relevance model. If that model is unavailable, the workflow falls back to the combined retrieval score.

### 24.4 Entailment prioritization

Retrieved excerpts may be prioritized using a dedicated entailment model. If that model is unavailable, strict language-model verification is used later.

### 24.5 Eligibility floor

Only excerpts meeting the configured minimum support score are eligible to substantiate a supported verdict. Excerpts below the floor are excluded from the authoritative support context used for scoring.

## 25. Stage 8: Dimension-Level Grounded Validation

Each mitigation measure is assessed across six dimensions.

### 25.1 Hazard fit

Question: Does the measure address the selected hazard?

Support requires an excerpt linking the intervention or its underlying mechanism to reducing the selected harm or outcome. The excerpt does not need to name the exact programme, population, or geography.

### 25.2 Mechanism

Question: Does the corpus support the causal pathway by which the measure is expected to reduce harm?

Support may come from excerpts describing component steps or the underlying mechanism, even when the exact implementation is not named. A pathway must not be invented when the corpus is silent.

### 25.3 Justification soundness

Question: Is the user's reasoning internally coherent, and is it free from contradiction by the corpus?

This dimension assesses the complete frozen justification. It may be supported without an external citation when the reasoning is understandable and internally coherent and no excerpt contradicts it.

### 25.4 Evidence quality

Question: Does the selected authoritative corpus provide relevant and adequate coverage for this specific measure and hazard?

For the user-evidence branch, the supplied evidence itself is assessed. For the curated-knowledge branch, the adequacy of curated coverage is assessed. Absence of user evidence is not counted against a proposal evaluated through the curated-knowledge branch.

### 25.5 Contraindications

Question: Does the corpus identify a conflict, risk, or incompatibility?

A contraindication is contradicted when the corpus identifies a conflict. It is supported only when the corpus affirmatively establishes that the measure is conflict-free. Silence is recorded as insufficient information, not as proof of safety.

### 25.6 Feasibility

Question: Does the corpus support practical applicability of the measure?

Silence on feasibility is recorded as insufficient information rather than inferred support.

## 26. Dimension Verdict Rules

Each dimension receives one of three verdicts:

### 26.1 Supported

The dimension's assessment test is met by eligible authoritative material.

Supported verdicts require at least one valid citation, except that justification soundness may be supported based on internal coherence and absence of contradiction.

### 26.2 Contradicted

An eligible authoritative excerpt actively conflicts with the measure or justification on that dimension. A contradiction must cite the conflicting excerpt.

### 26.3 Insufficient information

The authoritative corpus neither establishes nor contradicts the dimension. No citation is attached because no excerpt meets the support or contradiction test.

Insufficient information is a neutral coverage finding, not an implied rejection.

## 27. Stage 9: Repeated Assessment and Verdict Stability

Grounded validation is performed multiple times to reduce dependence on a single model response.

### 27.1 Standard sampling

Three independent dimension-level assessments are performed under the default configuration.

### 27.2 Contradiction confirmation

When any assessment identifies a contradiction, two additional assessments are performed for the affected dimensions.

A contradiction becomes the final dimension verdict only when the proportion of contradiction findings meets the configured confirmation threshold. Under the default configuration, the threshold is forty percent.

An unconfirmed contradiction is conservatively converted to insufficient information rather than treated as support.

### 27.3 Majority aggregation

Where no confirmed contradiction exists:

- supported must outnumber the combined insufficient-information and unconfirmed-contradiction findings to become the final verdict;
- otherwise, the final verdict is insufficient information.

### 27.4 Verdict stability

Verdict stability represents the average proportion of assessments agreeing with the final dimension verdict. It contributes to the overall confidence score.

## 28. Stage 10: Overall Outcome Logic

The operational final gate treats **hazard fit** and **justification soundness** as the critical dimensions.

The mechanism dimension is assessed and reported as a major grounding dimension, but under the current operational decision rule it does not independently block acceptance when the two final-gate critical dimensions are supported and no contradiction exists.

### 28.1 Reject

The mitigation measure is rejected when any applicable dimension is contradicted.

A contradiction is a hard veto, including contradictions in caution-oriented dimensions.

### 28.2 Abstain

The workflow abstains when there is no confirmed contradiction, but one or more final-gate critical dimensions have insufficient information.

Abstention means the current authoritative corpus cannot validate the measure. It is not a claim that the measure is wrong.

### 28.3 Pass

The mitigation measure passes when:

- no applicable dimension is contradicted; and
- all final-gate critical dimensions are supported.

Insufficient information in non-critical dimensions is retained as a caution in the final result.

## 29. Stage 11: Confidence Scoring

The grounding confidence score combines:

- **critical-dimension coverage**, weighted at sixty percent;
- **retrieval support strength**, weighted at twenty-five percent; and
- **verdict stability**, weighted at fifteen percent.

Critical-dimension coverage is the proportion of final-gate critical dimensions marked supported.

Retrieval support strength is based on eligible citation scores for supported dimensions.

The score summarizes grounding confidence. It does not replace the reject, abstain, or pass decision rules.

## 30. Stage 12: Outcome Handling

### 30.1 Rejected outcome

When rejected:

- contradicted dimensions are identified;
- explanations and cited conflicting excerpts are shown;
- temporary user evidence is discarded; and
- the user is asked to revise the measure, justification, or evidence.

### 30.2 Abstained outcome

When the workflow abstains:

- critical coverage gaps are identified;
- the user's current reason and evidence are preserved;
- the user may revise the justification or add a published source; and
- no validated mitigation measure is recorded yet.

### 30.3 Passed outcome

When passed:

- a grounded synthesis is generated;
- readable user evidence is preserved in a quarantined validated-evidence area;
- the measure, justification, validation results, and synthesis are recorded; and
- the user proceeds to review and subsequent evaluation.

## 31. Stage 13: Grounded Synthesis

A passed mitigation measure receives a concise synthesis built only from:

- the frozen measure description;
- the frozen justification;
- the selected hazard;
- the affected groups; and
- eligible authoritative excerpts.

### 31.1 Atomic claim requirement

The synthesis is decomposed into short atomic claims. Every claim must either:

- cite at least one eligible authoritative excerpt; or
- explicitly identify the validated user field it faithfully restates.

Unsupported recommendations, benefits, risks, groups, mechanisms, and limitations are omitted.

### 31.2 Entailment verification

Each proposed synthesis claim is checked to confirm that its cited excerpts or named validated fields directly support the full claim.

Claims that require indirect inference, general knowledge, or unsupported extrapolation are removed.

### 31.3 Caution reporting

Non-critical dimensions with insufficient information are presented as cautions so that acceptance is not mistaken for complete evidence coverage.

## 32. Evidence Governance

### 32.1 Temporary evidence

Uploaded or fetched evidence is held temporarily while clarification and validation are in progress.

### 32.2 Validated evidence

After a passed user-evidence assessment:

- temporary evidence is moved to a quarantined validated-evidence area;
- provenance, validation time, and analysis session are recorded; and
- inline evidence may also be admitted to the quarantined area.

### 32.3 Discarded evidence

Temporary evidence is discarded when:

- clarification cannot be completed within the allowed rounds; or
- the mitigation measure is rejected because of confirmed contradiction.

### 32.4 Corpus isolation

Validated user evidence is quarantined rather than automatically merged into the curated main knowledge base. This prevents one user's evidence from silently becoming authoritative for unrelated future assessments.

## 33. Mitigation Workflow Decision Summary

| Decision point | Proceed when | Return or stop when |
|---|---|---|
| Measure screening | A meaningful action can be identified | Blank, gibberish, too short, or not an action |
| Duplicate review | Measure is distinct, or user deliberately continues | User chooses to replace it after reviewing an existing measure |
| Justification review | Claimed effect or pathway is understandable | Missing, too short, or unclear |
| Evidence readability | No evidence, or readable evidence exists | Supplied evidence cannot be read |
| Clarification | Intended meaning can be frozen | Ambiguity remains after five rounds |
| Grounded validation | No contradiction and critical dimensions supported | Contradiction causes rejection; critical gaps cause abstention |
| Synthesis | Claims are directly entailed by evidence or validated inputs | No reliable grounded claims can be produced |
| Recording | Validation passes and synthesis succeeds | Rejected, abstained, or validation unavailable |

# Part C: Quality Assurance and Auditability

## 34. Separation of Decision Responsibilities

The methodology uses distinct checks for:

- input meaningfulness;
- ambiguity;
- duplicate detection;
- evidence retrieval;
- evidence relevance;
- dimension-level groundedness;
- contradiction confirmation;
- overall outcome calculation; and
- final-claim entailment.

This separation reduces the risk that one broad model judgement controls the entire decision.

## 35. Citation Controls

- Citations must refer to excerpts present in the selected authoritative corpus.
- Invented or unavailable citation identifiers are removed.
- Supported and contradicted verdicts without required valid citations are converted to insufficient information.
- Citations below the minimum support-score threshold cannot substantiate support.

## 36. User Control and Revision

At all revision points, the user retains control over the substantive proposal. The workflow may:

- identify ambiguity;
- ask reflective questions;
- identify overlap;
- explain contradictions;
- identify evidence gaps; and
- produce a clarified restatement.

It must not silently replace the user's intended hazard or mitigation measure with a different proposal.

## 37. Records Produced

### 37.1 Accepted hazard record

An accepted hazard record includes:

- hazard description;
- selected country, region, and sector context;
- source classification as user-added;
- user reason;
- optional evidence; and
- subsequent affected-population information.

### 37.2 Accepted mitigation record

An accepted mitigation record includes:

- association with the selected hazard;
- validated measure description;
- validated justification;
- dimension-level validation results;
- support-corpus classification;
- confidence and stability information;
- grounded synthesis; and
- relevant target or affected-group information used in later evaluation.

## 38. Interpretation of Outcomes

- Hazard acceptance means the regional hazard is meaningful, non-duplicative, contextually relevant, and compatible with the available sector statistical context.
- Mitigation pass means the selected authoritative corpus supports the operational critical dimensions and contains no confirmed contradiction.
- Mitigation abstention means evidence coverage is insufficient for validation.
- Mitigation rejection means the authoritative corpus contains a confirmed conflict.
- None of these outcomes constitutes a complete policy impact evaluation. Passed mitigation measures still proceed to subsequent evaluation.

## 39. Methodological Limitation to Document

The mitigation assessment describes mechanism as a critical grounding concern, but the current final outcome gate operationally requires support for hazard fit and justification soundness. Mechanism remains assessed and visible, yet insufficient mechanism information alone does not currently force abstention.

This distinction should be explicitly retained in reporting and reviewed as a governance decision if the methodology is revised.
