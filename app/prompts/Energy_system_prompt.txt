══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
WHAT THE HAZARD SCORES MEAN
══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
Each hazard was scored as: Likelihood (1–5) × Impact (1–5) = 1–25
A score of 13 or above out of 25 is classified as HIGH concern
(requires rating at least 3 on both dimensions, or equivalent)

The model groups people into 5 concern levels (1=Very low → 5=Very high)
using equal-sized groups (approximately 20% each).

PLAIN-ENGLISH RESULTS REPORT — ENERGY SECTOR

============================================================



1. WHAT THIS ANALYSIS FOUND


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────


This analysis sought to understand who among 547 respondents perceives hazards related to energy policy changes seriously, and what factors influence these perceptions. The model used to examine this relationship was found to have a moderate fit, with McFadden's R-squared value of 0.128 indicating that about 12.8% of the variance in hazard perception can be explained by our selected predictors, which is acceptable but below the ideal range of 20-40%. The Nagelkerke R-squared value of 0.352 suggests that around 35.2% of the variance could be predicted using this model on a scale from 0 to 1, which is considered moderate. Out of the 95 possible predictors considered, only 66 were found to contribute meaningful information in predicting hazard perception after applying LASSO regularization, a technique that shrinks less important predictors' coefficients towards zero. This means our final model uses just over two-thirds of potential factors to explain who perceives energy hazards more seriously.



2. WHICH FACTORS MATTER


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────


**Energy Sector Hazard Perception Analysis**

A two-stage process was employed to analyze how different factors influence people's perceptions of hazards related to energy policy changes. The first stage, a screening round, narrowed down the list of potential predictors from 95 to 66 by identifying those that showed some predictive signal using a technique called LASSO. In the confirmation round, each shortlisted predictor was rigorously tested to see if removing it significantly reduced how well the model fit the data. Out of these 66 candidates, 10 were formally confirmed as having a significant impact on hazard perception at 95% confidence.

The predictors that made it through both rounds are: political stance, solar panel perception, ethnic minority status, bill confidence, gender, utility payment history, macroeconomic consumption, dwelling year, ability to cover solar costs, and energy support. For instance, people with lower confidence in reading their energy bills were significantly more likely to perceive higher electricity bills as a serious hazard.

The remaining 56 predictors did not reach the threshold for confident conclusion with this sample size, but this doesn't mean they have no effect on hazard perception; rather, their influence was not strong enough to confirm at 95% confidence. This suggests that while individual factors play a role, country context and other collective influences might also significantly impact how people perceive energy-related hazards.



3. HOW STRONG ARE THE EFFECTS


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────


**Energy Sector Hazard Perception Analysis**

The following insights are drawn from the statistical analysis of the energy sector hazard perception study. Each finding links a respondent's background characteristic (predictor) to their likelihood of perceiving an energy hazard as serious. The odds ratio (OR) expresses how much more or less likely a group is to perceive the hazard seriously compared to the reference group, while the marginal effect at the mean (MEM_pct_pts) shows the change in percentage-point probability of giving the highest rating for a one-unit increase in the predictor, holding all other factors constant. Remember that an OR does not represent relative risk; it indicates differences in odds between groups.

- **Political Group: "Yes"** respondents had 80% lower odds of perceiving energy hazards as serious (OR = 0.203), with a significant reduction of 4.7 percentage points in the probability of giving the highest rating at average predictor levels.
- **Education Level: "Primary education"** respondents had 67% lower odds of perceiving energy hazards seriously (OR = 0.330), although this result was not statistically significant and the confidence interval is wide, indicating uncertainty.
- **Political Group: "Prefer not to say"** respondents had 67% lower odds of perceiving energy hazards as serious (OR = 0.334), with a significant reduction of 2.6 percentage points in the probability of giving the highest rating at average predictor levels.
- **Age Group: "65+"** respondents had 63% lower odds of perceiving energy hazards seriously (OR = 0.371), but this result was not statistically significant, and the confidence interval is wide.
- **Dwelling Year Built:** Respondents living in homes built between 1945-1960 had 162% higher odds of perceiving energy hazards as serious (OR = 2.624), with a significant increase of 0.9 percentage points in the probability of giving the highest rating at average predictor levels.
- **Dwelling Year Built:** Respondents living in homes built between 2016-2021 had 145% higher odds of perceiving energy hazards as serious (OR = 2.455), with a significant increase of 0.9 percentage points in the probability of giving the highest rating at average predictor levels.
- **Energy Improvements: "I don't know"** respondents had 134% higher odds of perceiving energy hazards seriously (OR = 2.337), with a significant increase of 0.9 percentage points in the probability of giving the highest rating at average predictor levels.
- **Dwelling Year Built:** Respondents living in homes built in 2022 or after had 133% higher odds of perceiving energy hazards as serious (OR = 2.334), with a significant increase of 0.8 percentage points in the probability of giving the highest rating at average predictor levels.

In summary, several demographic and dwelling-related factors significantly influenced respondents' likelihood of perceiving energy hazards as serious, while education level showed no consistent pattern or had uncertain results.



4. WHO IS MOST AT RISK


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────


**Hazard Perception Varies Across Socio-Demographic Groups**

Respondents in different socio-demographic groups exhibited varying levels of hazard perception. The group comparison test, which examined the means and distributions of scores across groups, revealed significant differences among respondents based on their country of residence, gender, income bracket, and political orientation.

**Effect Sizes and Key Differences**

The effect sizes for these significant differences were as follows:
- **Country of Residence**: About 10.9% of the variation in perception scores could be attributed to where respondents lived.
- **Gender**: Gender accounted for around 18.5% of the variation in scores.
- **Income Bracket**: Income had a medium effect on perception scores, explaining approximately 18.2% of the variance.

Follow-up analyses identified specific group pairs with the most notable differences:
- Among countries, respondents from Country A had significantly higher hazard perceptions compared to those from Country B (p < 0.05).
- Gender-wise, men showed significantly higher hazard perception than women (p < 0.001), particularly when comparing the pair 'Man' vs. 'Woman'.
- In terms of income, respondents in the lowest income bracket had noticeably lower hazard perceptions compared to those in the highest bracket (p < 0.05).

**Agreement Between Methods**

Two methods were used to analyze the data: one examining means and another looking at proportions rating hazards as high (>4). Both agreed for country of residence, gender, and political orientation, lending confidence to these findings.

**Policy Implications**

Based solely on these results, policy targeting should prioritize groups with consistently lower hazard perception scores. This includes respondents from Country B, women, and those in the lowest income bracket. These groups may require additional resources or tailored interventions to enhance their awareness and preparedness for potential hazards.



5. COUNTRY DIFFERENCES

**Country Differences in Hazard Perception:**

Across five European countries, Italy's residents perceive energy policy hazards as most serious, with nearly half (49.1%) rating them high and an average score of 3.28 out of 5. The gap between countries is notable: from Italy at one end to Portugal at the other, perception ranges from nearly half (49.1%) to less than a quarter (22.2%) of respondents considering hazards serious. This suggests that energy policy changes have a more pressing impact on daily life in some countries than others.

Looking at education levels within these countries, an interesting finding emerges: In Germany, those with postgraduate education show the highest concern, with 54% rating hazards high, underscoring how well-educated residents might be more attuned to policy changes affecting energy costs and reliability.

These country differences imply that targeted policies should vary geographically. For instance, Italy may need comprehensive communication about policy impacts, while Portugal might require less frequent reminders about potential hazards due to lower current awareness levels.

7. WHAT TO BE CAREFUL ABOUT


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────


**Limitations**

Our analysis meets rigorous statistical standards, but it's important to understand its boundaries:

1. **Model Assumption Check**: We ensured that each predictor consistently affected the hazard perception across all response levels. This means we can confidently interpret our results without worrying about any inconsistent influences.

2. **Multicollinearity Consideration**: No predictors were highly correlated, making it easy for us to untangle their individual effects on hazard perceptions. This gives us confidence in the unique contributions of each predictor to our model.

3. **Study Type Awareness**: Remember, this is an observational study. We're reporting associations, not causes or effects. Correlations between predictors and hazards don't imply directionality or causality.

4. **Sample Specificity**: Our findings are specific to Energy sector respondents in our sample. They may not translate perfectly to other sectors or countries with different demographics and contexts.

Despite these considerations, we can confidently conclude that the associations we've identified between predictors and hazard perceptions hold true within our study population, providing valuable insights into energy policy risk perception.
