What FITTER-EU is

A Horizon Europe research project asking a question policy rarely asks in advance: if we push this transition measure, who is worse off afterwards — and what would we do about it?

Its full title is Fair and inclusive twin transitions for a stronger social Europe, coordinated by Technological University Dublin across ten partner organisations.

The distinctive contribution is anticipatory governance — moving the analysis of harm from after the fact to before the decision. Rather than measuring who was hurt in hindsight, the project builds an evidence base and a working tool that show the likely distribution of harm while a policy is still a draft.

It treats vulnerability as intersectional. People are rarely disadvantaged along one axis alone, and a measure that helps low-income households in general can still miss low-income rural households without a car, or older tenants who cannot act on a retrofit grant.

The twin transition, and why it needs two words

Europe is decarbonising and digitalising at the same time. The two are usually studied separately. FITTER-EU treats them as one interacting system — because they reinforce and undercut each other, and the overlap is where damage concentrates.

How the evidence was built

Nothing in Dr Transition's knowledge base is there by assertion. Each layer was produced by a defined method and published as an open deliverable.

01 · Defining a just transition, precisely enough to test

A systematic mapping of existing theory, approaches and indicators of eco-social justice, followed by a critical reworking of how the economy, the labour market and inequality itself are defined in EU policy language.

This produced the operational definitions everything downstream depends on: what counts as a disadvantaged group, what counts as a harm, and what makes a measure just rather than merely effective.

Systematic literature mapping · critical concept analysis · intersectional framing

02 · Mapping who actually decides, in each country

A normative model of what a just transition governance system should contain, tested against the real policies, institutions, actors and decision processes of six member states.

The comparison exposes gaps and bottlenecks, and shows which enablers of eco-social policy exist in one country and not another. This is why a measure that works in Ireland is not automatically portable to Hungary.

Governance framework review · policy and actor mapping across six national systems

03 · Establishing the baseline, then the hazards

National baseline scenarios were derived from each country's long-term strategies and action plans for meeting EU energy targets and Paris commitments — the trajectory already in motion. Vulnerabilities were mapped against that baseline in four sectors, using indicators and profiles of structurally vulnerable cohorts.

From the baseline, participatory future scenarios were built and assessed for hazards: the specific negative effects a transition policy can produce, the drivers behind them, and the groups exposed to them.

MLP-PESTLE analytical framework · stakeholder interviews · iterative Delphi surveys with experts

04 · Testing whether exposed people see the same hazards

A dedicated survey took the hazards identified in the hazards deliverable and put them to the public across the six countries in energy, housing and transport, asking how serious each one is considered to be.

Ordinal regression with national macro-level indicators identifies which socio-demographic and behavioural characteristics predict high perceived seriousness. Expert-identified risk and lived experience of risk are not always the same thing, and the gap between them is itself a finding.

Sector-stratified survey · ordinal regression · regularised predictor selection

05 · Writing measures with the people who carry the risk

Between September and December 2025, national laboratories ran in all six countries — in-person and online sessions with intermediary organisations representing disadvantaged groups, policymakers, policy experts and civil society actors.

Sessions used system mapping to trace how a transition harm actually propagates, then moved from that map to concrete policy proposals. Each country produced a portfolio of measures, some adapting existing policy and some entirely new.

Structured facilitation · systemic mapping · six national case studies

How measures were classified

Every measure produced in the labs was assessed on two axes and placed accordingly. This classification is what turns a portfolio into something usable rather than a wish list — and it is the same judgement Dr Transition asks you to think through.

Where the evidence applies

Four sectors were analysed for baseline vulnerabilities and hazards. Three carried through into the survey and the co-creation labs — and those three are where the knowledge base runs deepest.

Where your input goes

The co-creation labs did not end when the sessions closed — their outputs were structured to feed this platform. Dr Transition continues the same process with a wider set of contributors, and holds every proposal to the same evidential standard.

What the platform will not do: tell you a measure is sound because it sounds plausible. Every conclusion traces back either to the project's evidence base or to what you supplied and confirmed. When neither covers the question, the honest answer is that it cannot conclude — and that is the answer you get.
