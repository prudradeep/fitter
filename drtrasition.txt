1. What is Dr Transition / Fitter Digital Platform
Dr Transition is a conversational digital coach, developed within the FITTER-EU project, that helps end users reason about the negative consequences of green-and-digital (“twin”) transition policies in the energy, housing and transport sectors. It takes the form of a guided, chat-based session that an end user works through for a specific local context. Crucially, it is a pedagogical tool rather than a prescriptive one: it does not hand back a score or a recommendation, but works alongside the user to sharpen and document their own reasoning.
Its purpose. Transition policies that look beneficial in aggregate can quietly disadvantage particular groups — for example, by raising costs for those least able to absorb them, or by overlooking people without practical alternatives. Dr Transition exists to make those effects visible before a policy is adopted. Its aim is not to decide policy, but to help end users reach decisions that are fairer, better understood, and defensible — and to leave them more capable of reasoning this way the next time, with or without the tool.
What it does. Within a single session, the platform helps the user (1) pin down their exact context — country, region and sector; (2) identify the hazards and negative impacts relevant to that context, and the population groups most affected; (3) co-develop concrete mitigation measures and have each one checked against trusted evidence; (4) reflect on how transformative and how feasible each measure really is; and (5) walk away with a clear, downloadable report capturing the hazards, affected groups, measures and the reasoning behind them. Along the way it also lets the user extend the analysis to their own region — adding new hazards and newly affected groups that the shipped material in the platform does not cover. This is crucial from a platform sustainability standpoint.
How it does it. The core engine of the platform is conversational probing. Instead of evaluating a policy for the user, the platform interrogates the user’s own statements through a systems-thinking lens — challenging the assumptions behind a judgement, tracing knock-on effects across sectors and over time, and repeatedly asking who benefits and who is excluded. Every input the user offers — claims, justifications, and supporting evidence as documents or links — is checked against the platform’s authoritative knowledge before it is accepted, and anything that contradicts the project’s core principles is set aside. Proposed measures are clarified until their meaning is unambiguous, then validated against the evidence and labelled as supported, contradicted, or simply unproven, so the user always knows how firm the ground beneath a measure is.
The engine behind it. The means by which all of this is achieved is a large language model (LLM) — the same class of conversational artificial intelligence behind modern chat assistants. The LLM is what conducts the dialogue: it generates the probing questions, interprets the user’s free-text answers and uploaded evidence, and carries out the reasoning that checks each input and measure. What makes the platform trustworthy is that this model is deliberately fenced in. Rather than answering from its own training or opinions, it is required to reason against the platform’s curated knowledge: it retrieves the relevant trusted material and bases its judgements on that, and any claim it cannot ground in that evidence is flagged as unproven rather than asserted. Because AI judgement can be inconsistent, key checks are run several times and combined, and a conclusion is only accepted when it points to specific supporting evidence. In short, the LLM supplies the fluency and flexibility of natural conversation, while the platform’s knowledge and safeguards supply the discipline — so the user gets an interrogator that is articulate but kept honest.

Why this approach works. A tool that simply scored policies would hide its reasoning, invite over-reliance, and do nothing to improve the user’s own judgement. The coaching approach does the opposite. By making the user articulate and defend their thinking, it surfaces blind spots that a number would conceal; by grounding every conclusion in cited evidence and refusing to treat missing evidence as proof, it stays honest; by adapting its questioning to each region and sector, it respects that no two policy contexts are alike; and by keeping the human firmly in control, it supports better decisions without displacing the decision-maker. The lasting product of a session is therefore not a verdict but a more rigorous, more equitable way of thinking — captured in a record others can review.
